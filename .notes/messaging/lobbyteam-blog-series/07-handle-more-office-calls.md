<p><strong>To handle more office calls without making customers wait, separate routine questions from requests that need a person, collect useful context before a handoff, and create a backup path for busy or after-hours periods. More phone capacity should mean more progress, not more messages sitting in a queue.</strong></p>

<p>Most offices do not have a call-volume problem every minute of the day. They have spikes: the first hour of the morning, lunch coverage, a schedule change, a weather event, or a busy afternoon when several people call at once.</p>

<h2>Design for the busy moment</h2>

<p>Ask what should happen when the receptionist is already helping someone. A useful call workflow can:</p>

<ol>
<li>Give a clear answer to approved routine questions.</li>
<li>Collect the reason for the call instead of only a name and number.</li>
<li>Offer a configured next step, such as an appointment request or callback.</li>
<li>Route a specialist request with the context the next person needs.</li>
<li>Tell the caller what will happen if the preferred person is unavailable.</li>
</ol>

<p>This is not the same as placing every caller into an automated maze. The design should make it easier to reach a human when that is the right outcome.</p>

<h2>Use a queue for work, not just calls</h2>

<p>A call log tells you that a call happened. A useful queue tells the team what needs to happen next.</p>

<p>For each message, record the request, urgency, preferred channel, relevant account or appointment, and the owner. If the request is routed to another person, include the original question and the information already collected.</p>

<h2>Protect the receptionist's attention</h2>

<p>The front desk should not have to answer the same basic question while a visitor is standing in front of them. Approved answers can cover the predictable requests. A receptionist can take over when the customer needs nuance, reassurance, negotiation, or a decision outside the rules.</p>

<p>The goal is not to make the human interaction less personal. It is to stop routine interruption from consuming all of the human team's attention.</p>
